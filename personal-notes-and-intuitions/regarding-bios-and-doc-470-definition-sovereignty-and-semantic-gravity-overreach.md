Chatgpt: Yes. That is the missing precision. The clamp cannot be arbitrary. It must be built around an already descriptive function or dependency of the subject.
A clamp is legitimate when it protects or stabilizes something the subject already does, needs, depends on, or is exposed to.
Not:
I prefer this rule, so I install a clamp.
But:
This subject already has a real function, pressure, dependency, or failure surface. The clamp is specified to keep that function usable without letting it become destructive.
That gives you a strong rule:
A constructive clamp must begin from a descriptive function before it becomes a prescription.
Or sharper:
Prescription is legitimate only when it is traceable to a descriptive load.
The pattern
For every clamp, ask:
1. What does the subject already do or need?
 Communication, breathing, eating, sheltering, trading, bonding, coordinating, producing tools, making plastics, seeking safety, forming categories.
2. What makes that function valuable or necessary?
 Communication lets local ends become shareable. Plastics solve real material problems. Biology gives us capacity and limits. Environment sustains the floor.
3. What happens when it is under-clamped?
 Too little structure, no accounting, no limits, no trust, no correction.
4. What happens when it is over-clamped?
 Too much control, dogma, prohibition, capture, domination, paralysis.
5. What clamp preserves the function without owning it?
That is the whole engineering grammar.

Plastics example
This is a great case.
Humans need and want plastics because plastics serve real functions:
• sanitation
• medicine
• packaging
• insulation
• electronics
• transport
• preservation
• affordability
• durability
• lightweight materials
• safety equipment
So “plastics are bad” is too crude.
The descriptive truth is:
humans have real reasons to make plastics.
But the other descriptive truth is:
plastics can persist, accumulate, fragment, contaminate ecosystems, burden bodies, damage habitats, and exceed the environment’s absorption capacity.
So the clamp is not:
abolish all plastics.
And it is not:
make infinite plastics because humans find them useful.
The clamp is:
preserve the valid human functions plastics serve while limiting production, disposal, toxicity, persistence, and ecological overload.
Too thin:
• unlimited production
• disposable culture
• externalized waste
• microplastic accumulation
• ecosystem burden
• corporate non-accounting
Too thick:
• total bans that destroy medical, sanitary, or practical uses
• ignoring affordability and replacement burdens
• moral purity over material transition
• banning before substitutes exist
Healthy clamp:
make plastics where their function is hard to replace and their lifecycle can be accounted for; reduce, redesign, substitute, or ban uses where the environmental burden exceeds the human function.
A concise formulation:
Plastics are not dangerous because making things is wrong. They become dangerous when their useful function is allowed to exceed the environment’s capacity to absorb their costs.
Or even cleaner:
The plastic clamp must preserve the human function while preventing the material residue from eating the floor that function depends on.
